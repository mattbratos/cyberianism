# Why Essays Suck (But Shouldn't)
I really love reading and writing essays, and I think it's sad that they're so underrated. This is probably because of how, and by whom, we're introduced to the art of essay writing in school. 

### How School Kills the Essay
First, let's talk about the "how." Picture your English teacher (who probably peaked in high school) droning on about five-paragraph structures and topic sentences. Rules upon rules that turn essay writing into an exercise in creative suffocation. The majority of topics in school are also dreadfully stupid. Another issue is that you can't genuinely express your own opinions in school essays, which makes the writing process even more agonizing.

### Example
Now let's grab some random school topic from the Internet and try to write an essay about it. Alright, I've found a topic: 'What inspires you?'So here's what I'm thinking:

My biggest inspirations are probably hip-hop, art-house cinema, books, essays, philosophy, acid, alcohol, and boobs. What does it even mean to be 'inspired'? According to the Internet, the definition is: 'fill (someone) with the urge or ability to do or feel something, especially to do something creative.' I'm lucky to know a lot of creative people in the art industry, and I can promise you that their source of inspiration is most often purely chemical. I also don't think that this question is a good one. Inspires you to do what? Why does it even assume that I'm inspired in any way right now?

### F for Freedom of Speech
So, yeah, that's about it. I'd probably get an 'F' for this essay. If I'm not allowed to write the truth, then I'm forced to lie just to survive. The whole point of an essay is to restructure your thinking by putting it down on paper, to make sense of things, and to clear your mind. Having to guess what lies will make someone you don't care about happy, just to get an 'A,' is pure torture.

### E for Example
Let's not ignore the "who" part of the equation. Let's face it, most teachers are not the brightest people you will meet. At least mine weren't. Even if they are their minds are right by some stupid system that doesn't allow them to teach how they want. Also, it's not like you have a right to choose not to write them. Forcing people to do stuff they don't want to can make even the most pleasant things seem horrible. If teachers say, 'Get educated so you can be like me,' then I've been doing everything to avoid becoming like them.

# The Power of Essays
Essays are basically tools for changing the world. If you think about how our society progressed in history every major disruption is caused by an essay or collection of them. Examples? Here they are: 

* WW2 > Mein Kampf
* Christianity > Bibble 
* American Independence > Common Sense
* Feminism > The Second Sex
* The Information Age > The GNU Manifesto 
* "I Have a Dream" speech > Civil Rights Movement
* The Republic > Foundation of Western philosophy
* The Wealth of Nations > Birth of modern economics

So, you see, essays have been ripping apart the fabric of society and stitching it back together for centuries. Even if you don't know about it your actions, decisions and world views are shaped by Platon, Nietzsche and Jesus. Some parts of their essays are still living inside you. But here's the kicker: traditionally, once an essay was inked, it was set in stone. Can't edit it, can't update it—no patches for The Wealth of Nations or Bible, right?

Enter the Internet. It's set to turn the essay game upside down. How? Through open-source essays. No more lone Zarathustras meditating in caves for decades before dropping a text bomb. Soon, essays are going to be living, breathing documents, evolving in real-time with collective brainpower. We're witnessing the democratization of the most potent weapon in history—the written word. In the future, you won't have to be a genius to make an impact on the world. Everyone will be able to submit a pull request to the next Bible. So if essays are the engines of evolution, then the evolution of essays is the evolution of evolution. How fucking amazing is that?

# The future: Essay 2.0
I really don't understand why open-source essays haven't gotten extremely popular by now. It seems so obvious to do it that way, but for some reason, nobody does. I'm really trying to think of the advantages of closed-source essays, and I can't come up with anything. Open source, by its nature and design, acknowledges that the author/authors of a project have made mistakes in their work and put it on GitHub to let others point out and fix these errors. Are authors of modern essays so confident in their thinking that they don't believe it's even possible to improve their work? Or has nobody thought about it this way? Or maybe I have some blind spots, and there are obvious reasons why this isn't a good idea. Idk. Anyway, I will try to defend my thesis, you can use GitHub features to point out my blind spots (while simultaneously proving I'm right, so be careful). Anyway, here are some reasons why open-source essays on GitHub are just superior to regular ones.
 
1. Form    
I love PG and Sam Altman's essays, but reading them on their websites without dark mode in the middle of the night often burns my eyes. Also, idk why, but it really makes me disproportionately furious that the text is not responsive. Reading them on the computer is hard, but on the phone, it's like torture. GitHub UI is just so much more pleasant. I give them an A+ for content and a D- for presentation. (I know, I know, I'm just a spoiled Gen Z kid, and in your times, you read essays from papyrus in the torchlight, but don't kill the messenger, you spoiled us with a great software UI.)

2. Collaborative Feedback  
I don't know about you, but when I've spent like 10 hours on an essay, it means I'm interested in the topic and want to share my thoughts with the world. Traditional essays do that, sure, but I also want to know where my thinking is flawed and where I've made mistakes. PG probably gets a ton of positive feedback, but it's way harder to send an email saying, "Hi PG, loved your essay, but here's a mistake due to XYZ." No one invited you to critique, and it could come off as rude. On GitHub, the process is way smoother. Anyone can fork your essay, suggest changes, and submit a pull request. It's easier to give and accept feedback this way. Plus, you can clearly see which feedback was accepted and which was rejected. Finally, you can tune into discussions about your work and learn from them. Most of these conversations, in real life, happen behind the author's back.

3. Version Control  
Ever write something, delete it, and then regret it? GitHub's got your back with version control. Watch your essay evolve over time, and if needed, revert to an older draft. Also, it gives other people a chance to see how your thinking evolved over time. 

4. GitHub Issues for Logical Fallacies  
Find a flaw in the argument. Open an issue and get it patched in the next update.

5. Merge Conflicts Over Censorship  
In the academic world, certain ideas may get suppressed. In the open-source community, it’s the better argument that wins. If there’s disagreement, let's resolve it like adults: with a merged conflict.

6. AI Help  
I started writing essays in VS code when I was doing some side coding projects and I made a todo.txt file while writing shit to do I found out that Co-Pilot is super useful for writing in general and I often brainstorm with it. (like for example the argument above is made by AI, and I have no idea what Merge Conflicts are and how do they work but it sounded smart so I left it there. Hopefully, some smart hackers will explain it to me and decide to change, upgrade or delete this argument)

7. Learning by Doing   
The process teaches you Git and Markdown. For noob hackers, it can be actually really useful. 

8. Repository Stars as Social Proof   
The more stars your essay gets, the more people are likely to take it seriously. It's like academic citations but cooler.

9. Branching Arguments   
Have a tangent or an alternative viewpoint? Create a new branch for that argument without messing with the main essay.

10. Open Source Licenses   
Want people to use your work but also want credit? Slap an open-source license on there.

11. Memes and GIFs   
Academic journals don't allow this. Websites do allow it but you can't for example make a pull request with some cool meme you found that you think will be perfect for some essay. GitHub doesn’t. Need I say more?

12. Community Building   
Regular contributors can become maintainers. Your essay becomes a living, evolving community project, who knows maybe I will even finally get a real job out of it.

13. Translatability    
Community contributors can easily fork and translate your essay, making it accessible to a non-English speaking audience.
